American Consumers Cannot Afford Trump’s Trade Policy any Longer

Trump postponed his order to add an additional ten percent tariff on low-value goods imported from China to April 1.

By

February 17, Trump readdressed on X that he will charge “a reciprocal tariff for the purpose of fairness.” He couldn’t make it more clearer: everything is about reciprocity, a matter of ensuring that other nations do not take economic advantage of the United States.

Trump’s economic nationalism had been heavy on rhetoric. In his campaign, He promised to impose tariffs of at least 60 percent against Chinese imports if reelected. As expected, he did not hesitate to impose an additional ten percent tariff on imports from China and Hong Kong right after he took office.

One of the most significant changes is the revocation of the “de minimis” exception, which previously allowed packages valued under $800 to enter the U.S. duty-free.

Seemingly a strategic move to prevent imports from “exploiting” the “de minimis” exemption, trade friction has aggravated. This policy shift has sent shockwaves through e-commerce platforms like Amazon, Temu, and Shein, where millions of Americans turn for affordable household goods.

Couriers such as UPS and DHL soon stopped accepting parcels from China and Hong Kong briefly, sending out warnings about delays and potential tax charges. Meanwhile, millions of imported packages pile up at the customs, leaving them unprepared for inspection or paperwork within such a short period of time due to a lack of clearance logistics.

Less than two weeks later, Trump postponed the decision until April 1.

Under Trump's second administration, trade partners are better prepared. In response to a 10% U.S. tariff, China imposed a 15% retaliatory tariff on American coal and liquefied natural gas the same day.

Trump has long used brinkmanship in trade negotiations. In 2018, the U.S. imposed a 25% tariff on Chinese industries like robotics, aerospace, and automobiles, worth $50 million. A few months later, China retaliated with the same tariff on U.S. soybeans, pork, and aircraft.

His goal to reduce trade deficits, however, did not yield the intended results. In 2018, Trump broke the record, resulting in a trade deficit of 417.3 billions owed to China.
